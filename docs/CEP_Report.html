Summary. An AI agent can only read a limited amount of text before it answers, and a retriever usually hands it far more than fits. This report describes cbm, a Context Budget Manager written in C++17 with our own binary heap, merge sort, linked list and hash map. We compared recency-first, relevance-first and relevance-per-token selection with a policy of our own, hybrid fresh-cover, which removes replaced notices and then repeatedly takes the passage offering the most new evidence per square root of its token cost.

On 32 labelled questions the hybrid covered 0.792, 0.908 and 1.000 of the needed facts at 256, 512 and 1,024 tokens, ahead of the best baseline at each budget (0.631, 0.842, 0.967), and never selected a replaced notice or a redundant copy. It went over budget zero times in 24,704 runs, as did every other policy. The price is real: it is roughly two and a half times slower than the baselines at normal query size and uses six to ten times their memory. Relevance-first also answered more facts on multi-source questions at 512 tokens. Answer quality was measured with an offline proxy, not a real language model.

1. Problem formulation

Picture a student asking the university's assistant for the last date of course registration. Before it answers, the agent receives course policies, notices, FAQ entries and pieces of earlier conversation, often several times more text than it may send to the model. Something has to decide what goes in. If that step is careless the agent can overflow its limit, lose the one fact that mattered, or quote a notice that was replaced last week and sound completely sure about it.

The brief asks one driving question: how should an agent represent, prioritise, select and order competing passages when memory, token capacity, relevance, time sensitivity and computing effort all limit what it can send? Our system begins after an existing retriever has supplied the candidates and ends with an ordered package that is guaranteed to fit. We did not train a model, build embeddings or write a vector database, since the brief places these outside the system boundary.

Capacity is calculated the way the brief defines it. From the total allocation Btotal we reserve Rfixed for instructions and Ranswer for the reply, which leaves

BCTX = max(0, Btotal − Rfixed − Ranswer)   and every selection S must satisfy   Σi∈S ci ≤ BCTX

In our experiments Rfixed = 300 and Ranswer = 500, so Btotal values of 1,056, 1,312 and 1,824 give context budgets of 256, 512 and 1,024 tokens. The cost ci is a nonnegative integer from the test fixture: body tokens plus 6 for the source label and separator. Costs in the main set run from 16 to 375 tokens, with a mean of 72.3 and a median of 54. No character-count estimate is used to claim that a limit was met.

2. Assumptions and constraints

We assume relevance scores in [0, 1] come from the retriever, freshness is an ISO timestamp, and a newer passage declares what it replaces through a supersedes link. Evidence tags of the form key:value are treated as trusted metadata. The last two assumptions are the weakest, and Section 12 returns to them.

The constraints of the brief were treated as fixed. Passages are indivisible, so an item goes in whole or not at all. Mandatory passages go first, and if they cannot all fit the system returns INFEASIBLE with a diagnostic instead of quietly dropping one. All four policies share one feasibility check and one tie chain (relevance descending, then timestamp descending, then passage id ascending), which makes the output deterministic for a fixed input. Every selected item keeps its passage id, source id, cost, priority and position. All data is local, and no network or model API is called.

3. Stakeholder trade-offs

The three stakeholder groups in the brief want things that pull against each other, and each conflict changed the design.

Students against operators. Students want answers backed by evidence, which argues for more text; operators want token spending controlled. We stopped maximising raw relevance and started maximising new evidence per token, so the budget buys facts instead of repetition. The hybrid uses slightly less of the budget than relevance-first at 512 tokens (97.8 % against 98.5 %) because it would rather leave tokens unspent than add a copy that says nothing new. Relevance-first selected 82 redundant passages there and the hybrid none.

Policy officers against relevance. Officers need current, traceable rules to outrank old ones, but old notices and their forum reposts score 0.78 to 0.95 while the revision scores only 0.62 to 0.80. A policy that trusts relevance will happily choose the old text. We therefore exclude replaced passages and their copies before selection starts, and we keep the source on every item. Relevance-first selected 56 stale passages at 512 tokens; the hybrid selected none.

Quality against speed and memory. Tracking covered facts and replaced notices needs hash maps, which cost time and memory, and operators care about both. We accepted the extra cost because it stays small in absolute terms at real query sizes: 16 µs against 6 to 7 µs at about 41 candidates. At n = 10,000 the difference is more visible, at 5.05 ms and 1.76 MB against 0.84 ms and 0.23 MB for a heap baseline. Operators can still predict it, because memory stays linear in n.

5. Data structure and algorithm design

5.1 Architecture

The system follows the separation suggested in the brief. A loader validates records, a policy engine selects, a validator recomputes the budget from the final ids, a packager orders the result and an agent adapter hands it on. These parts depend on each other more than the diagram suggests. The loader's duplicate-id rule is what makes a passage id safe to use as a hash key for stale detection, and the package order changes what a position-biased agent answers. The selector contains no model code, so a real language model could replace the offline agents without touching it.

5.3 The four policies

The three baselines differ only in their key: timestamp for recency-first, relevance for relevance-first, and relevance ÷ ci for relevance-per-token. Each places mandatory items, then walks the rest in key order and accepts whatever still fits. Each has a merge-sort engine and a heap engine, and the two always produced the same package (T15, 3,000 runs).

The hybrid responds to freshness and evidence coverage together, because the two interact: removing stale items frees budget for coverage, and discounting copies stops one long group crowding out other sources. A passage's weight combines its relevance with its source reliability (A 1.0, B 0.85, C 0.7). Its gain depends on what has already been chosen, so its priority falls as facts get covered.

w(i)        = relevance(i) × reliabilityWeight(i)
gain(i | S) = w(i) × (λ + (1−λ) × newTags(i,S)/|tags(i)|) × (dupPenalty if group(i) already in S)
priority(i) = gain(i | S) / max(c_i, 1)^α                     // α = 0.5, λ = 0.2, dupPenalty = 0.3

HYBRID_SELECT(cands, B_CTX):
  ids ← HashMap passage_id → index
  mark every passage replaced by a candidate, and every copy of it, as stale
  if Σ cost(mandatory) > B_CTX: return INFEASIBLE(diagnostic)
  accept every mandatory item; mark its tags covered and its group chosen
  items ← { (priority(i), i) : optional, not stale, c_i ≤ B_CTX }   // others omitted with a reason
  heap ← FLOYD_BUILD(items)
  while heap not empty and remaining ≥ minCost:
      (key, i) ← heap.pop()
      if c_i > remaining:              omit i "insufficient capacity"; continue
      if group chosen and no new tag:  omit i "duplicate, no new evidence"; continue
      p ← priority(i)                                         // true value now
      if p < key and heap.top() beats (p, i): heap.push((p, i)); continue
      accept i; remaining −= c_i; mark tags covered, group chosen
  package ← LINKED_LIST(accepted); LIST_MERGE_SORT(package, order rule)

5.4 Invariants and budget safety

  • Heap order. Parent ≥ child after every sift, checked with isHeap() under 20,000 random operations (T17).
  • Stored keys are upper bounds. As S grows, newTags can only fall and a group can only become chosen, so a gain never rises. A popped item that still beats the top is therefore truly the best, which is why the lazy result equals eager greedy (T16: 1,000 instances, 1,442 re-insertions, 0 mismatches).
  • Partition. Every candidate leaves through exactly one accept or one omit, so selected and omitted sets are disjoint and complete (T14).
  • No stale optional item. Stale passages are removed before the heap is built (T13, E08).

Why the budget can never be exceeded. Let r be the remaining capacity, starting at BCTX ≥ 0. If mandatory items exceed BCTX, nothing is accepted and the status is INFEASIBLE. Otherwise every acceptance in every policy goes through one function that accepts i only when ci ≤ r and then sets r ← r − ci. By induction r ≥ 0 after every step, so Σ c(S) = BCTX − r ≤ BCTX. A separate validator also recomputes the total from the final ids and would raise BUDGET_VIOLATION; it never fired.

The hybrid is a heuristic. Covering the most tags under a token budget is a budgeted maximum-coverage problem, which is NP-hard, and plain cost-scaled greedy has no constant-factor guarantee without the extra comparison described by Khuller et al. [5], which we did not implement.

The heap pays O(n) to build and a log n factor only for items it actually pops, so it is close to linear when k is much smaller than n, which is the normal case. As the budget grows, p approaches n and the heap's cost approaches the sort's. The hybrid adds an O(n) hashing pass with a large constant, because it hashes and copies strings. Section 8.4 tests each of these predictions.

7. Experimental setup

Main dataset. cbm gen (seed 2026) creates 32 labelled questions with 1,300 candidate passages: 10 time-sensitive policy questions, 10 multi-source questions, 10 conversation-memory questions and 2 with no answer in the data. Each policy scenario contains a revised notice, the old notice with three reposts that score higher, and irrelevant recent notices. Multi-source scenarios split four facts over four sources and add one long overview, near-duplicates and a contradicting forum post. The set holds 90 gold tags, 20 supersede links and 11 mandatory passages.

Labels. The generator writes each tag in the same step as the sentence stating it, and gold is the set of values stated by current official or FAQ passages. Copies carry the same tag and coverage is a set union, so repetition never counts as independent evidence. Passages are shuffled before ids are assigned, so an id reveals nothing. A separate code path (cbm validate) then checks that every tag value appears in its passage text, every gold tag is stated by a passage nothing replaces, and every supersede link points to an older passage. It reported 0 problems. Questions with no gold facts are left out of the coverage mean and scored only on whether the agent abstains.

MUET document set. We also ran the system on the corpus from our PBL project: 31 documents of the Department of Computer Systems Engineering, 155 passages and 20 hand-labelled questions. Every question receives all 155 passages, giving 3,100 records. Relevance here is BM25 scaled to the best passage, and cost is an estimate, ceil(1.3 × words) + 6, since no tokenizer was available. Three revised notices were linked to the originals they replace: attendance from 70 % to 75 %, exam dates, and registration from 10 to 17 September. These rules were mostly written by the team for testing, and only document D24 restates a real brief.

Scaling workloads. Synthetic sets of n = 100, 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 use the same cost mix, seed 20260000 + n, 20 % duplicates and 5 % superseded passages.

Protocol. The main comparison runs 32 questions × 3 budgets × 4 policies = 384 runs on identical input, with hybrid parameters fixed before evaluation. Timing uses std::chrono::steady_clock around the selector, with 1 warm-up and 5 timed runs per configuration (9 for scaling), and we report medians with ranges. The machine was an Intel Core i7-8650U (4 cores, 8 threads, 1.9 GHz base) with 15.8 GB RAM on Windows 11 Pro, compiled by TDM-GCC 10.3.0 with g++ -std=c++17 -O2 -Wall -Wextra and 0 warnings. The CPU was 43 % busy with other work during the runs, which explains the wide ranges. All metrics are recomputed from the selected ids by an evaluator separate from the selector.

9. Answer-quality analysis

We did not call a language model. Two deterministic extractive agents read only the context package. First-mention is position-biased and uses the first passage stating each fact; authority ignores order and prefers the most reliable, then newest, source. For each asked fact, the answer is correct if it gives the gold value, contradictory if it gives another value, and missing if it gives none. Because these agents only repeat what the context says, unsupported claims are zero by construction. This is a proxy for answerability, not observed LLM accuracy, and it should be read that way.

10. Engineering investigations

When is a long, relevant passage better than several short ones? When it fits with room to spare and states several needed facts that are not available more cheaply. The multi-source overview (260 to 380 tokens) never fits at 256. At 512, taking it gave relevance-first 0.825 and the α = 0 hybrid 0.875, against 0.825 for four short sources in the default hybrid and 0.525 for relevance-per-token. At 1,024 the short sources won outright: hybrid 1.000 against 0.900 for relevance-first, which spent its budget on the overview and copies.

When does newest-first lose evidence, and when does relevance-first include stale content? Newest-first fails when key facts are older than the chatter around them. Memory coverage was only 0.067, 0.133 and 0.533, and policy coverage stayed at 0.450 up to 512 because the 8 newest notices were irrelevant. Relevance-first includes stale content whenever old text outscores its revision, selecting 42, 56 and 60 stale passages across 19 to 20 questions.

Can relevance-per-token lose crucial evidence despite high utility? Yes, in 36 question and budget cases. On Q01 at 256 tokens it selected 6 passages with total relevance 4.114, including three cheap reposts of the old deadline, and missed the form name (coverage 1/2). The hybrid selected 5 passages with total relevance of only 3.157 and covered 2/2. Cheap copies look efficient but add no new evidence.

What dominates the runtime? For the sort engine it is the merge sort itself, 92 % of the time and independent of k. For the heap engine it is the O(n) build, then p log n as k grows. For the hybrid it is the hashing pass at 75 to 76 %, not the heap, with re-insertions never above 309. If the hybrid ever needs to be faster, the id handling is the place to start.

How should the system behave in awkward cases? If crucial items do not fit, it returns INFEASIBLE and names each mandatory id, its cost, the total and the budget (E05: E05-P1(150) + E05-P2(180) need 330 tokens but B_CTX = 256). An optional item larger than the budget is simply omitted while the rest are still chosen (E04). Replaced versions are excluded; other contradictions are kept with their source, and the authority agent prefers the more reliable one and flags the disagreement. Ties follow the fixed chain (E06, T09). In a near-duplicate group the hybrid keeps a copy only if it adds a new tag (E07: 2 passages and 2/2 coverage, against 5 copies and 1/2). A repeated copy could still be useful as corroboration from a separate source, but it would have to be scored as that, and we did not model it.

Does the order of the context change the answers? It depends on the agent. With the same selection, the authority agent's result did not change under any order (relevance-first at 512: 77/90). The first-mention agent did change: relevance-first scored 58/90 oldest-first, 67/90 by priority and 76/90 newest-first. The hybrid moved less, from 75 to 71, because its stale passages are already gone. Selection quality and order effects therefore need separate measurement, and order effects should be re-measured with a real model [6].

11. Verification and demonstration

cbm test runs 29 automated tests and all pass. They cover empty input, zero budgets, malformed records, duplicate ids, ties, heap and hash-map behaviour, and determinism. Three are property tests that give the strongest evidence: T14 checks budget safety and the partition rule on 24,000 random runs, T15 compares the heap and sort engines over 3,000 runs, and T16 compares lazy and eager greedy on 1,000 instances. With the 384 evaluation runs and 320 MUET runs, that makes 24,704 runs with no violation. The loader rejected all 15 malformed records in the fixture, each with a line number and a specific reason, such as negative token cost '-20' or duplicate passage id M01-P1 in M01 (first seen on line 2).

The edge cases required by the brief are in results/edge_cases.txt as E01 to E13. Two of them are worth noting because the hybrid gains nothing there. In E09, evidence split over three sources gives 1/3 for all four policies, because equally relevant distractors win. In E11 at 1,024 tokens, it ties recency-first and relevance-first at 4/4. A live run in the viva follows cbm select, cbm compare, an infeasible query on Q10, and cbm bench, and the interactive menu (cep/run_demo.bat) provides the same steps.

12. Limitations

  • Freshness depends on explicit links. A contradiction with no supersedes link goes undetected, and this causes the hybrid's remaining contradictory answers. A per-key "newest wins" rule over evidence tags is the obvious next step.
  • Coverage depends on the retriever. The hybrid cannot tell needed facts from distractors with the same relevance (E09).
  • No optimality guarantee. α = 0.5 under-weights a long multi-fact passage at mid budgets and can skip a needed longer passage at small ones (M09 at 128 tokens).
  • The answer measure is a proxy. Two simple agents stand in for a real model. A check with an LLM has not been done, so nothing here shows how a real model would answer.
  • Most data is synthetic or team-written. Absolute coverage numbers depend on how relevance and cost were designed, so the comparison between policies is the real finding. MUET costs are word-count estimates.
  • Noisy timing. Benchmarks ran on a laptop under 43 % load. Medians, ranges and repeated runs reduce this but do not remove it.

We know of no crashes or failing tests at the time of writing.

15. Conclusion

Returning to the driving question: an agent should treat each passage as an indivisible item with a cost and known evidence, settle mandatory items first, remove what has been replaced, and then choose by the new evidence each passage adds relative to its size. Our measurements support that approach. The hybrid led on coverage at every budget on the main set, kept out stale notices and copies, and never broke the budget.

It is not a universal winner. It costs more time and memory, it can lose to a long passage on multi-source questions, it can skip a needed notice at a very small budget, and it trusts metadata that a real system may not have. The clearest lesson for us was that the difficulty lies less in fitting text into a limit than in deciding which text deserves the space. The next real tests are a per-key freshness rule and an evaluation with an actual language model.
